Case study 01 · Applied analytics & community logistics

Balancing meal deliveries across ~18 volunteer drivers

During the COVID-19 response, food needed to reach about 200 delivery locations across a wide service area. I used k-nearest-neighbor proximity logic as a starting point, then shaped routes around equitable volunteer time, vehicle capacity, safer execution, and field feedback.

Zach Johnson FoundationSenior Program ManagerCOVID-19 response
~200delivery locations
~18volunteer drivers
100,000pounds distributed by the response

The challenge

Fairness mattered more than the shortest possible route.

About 18 people had volunteered their time to deliver food to roughly 200 locations. The goal was to give them routes with reasonably comparable time commitments, even when the routes could not have the same number of stops.

A dense neighborhood route could include more deliveries because the homes were close together and a larger vehicle could carry more boxes. A volunteer with limited cargo capacity might receive fewer stops, including distant locations that did not fit naturally into another route. The routing plan had to balance proximity with the realities of people, vehicles, and the work on the ground.

Routing concept

Start with proximity. Balance the whole assignment.

The diagram illustrates the method without showing actual family locations. Nearby stops created an analytical starting point; each route was then checked against time, stop density, vehicle capacity, construction, and safer delivery conditions.

Constraints in the field

Equity was designed into the operation.

Comparable time

Dense routes could carry more stops, while distant outliers could make a route longer with fewer deliveries.

Vehicle capacity

Larger vehicles could hold more boxes. Smaller vehicles received fewer stops so the assignment fit the space available.

Safer delivery

Routes were adjusted for construction, right-side drop-offs where practical, and volunteer safety feedback.

Service continuity

When a stop created a safety concern, the volunteer was reassigned and another delivery plan preserved support for the children in that household.

My approach

Translate data into assignments volunteers could actually use.

01

Create a proximity baseline

I used the address list and k-nearest-neighbor logic to identify stops that naturally belonged near one another.

02

Fit routes to people and vehicles

I adjusted the number and location of stops so expected time was roughly comparable and each volunteer could carry the assigned boxes.

03

Run a staggered hub

A local high school provided the space to unload the moving van and load volunteer vehicles. Pickups ran from about 7:30 to 8:30 a.m. in five- to ten-minute increments, avoiding a single large queue during COVID.

04

Learn and revise

Drivers could return to the hub with extra boxes or route feedback. I iterated as families joined or left, construction changed, safety concerns surfaced, and the team learned what worked.

The result

A repeatable delivery system volunteers enjoyed supporting.

The broader response distributed 100,000 pounds of food. About 18 drivers served roughly 200 locations through routes designed for comparable time commitments, even when mileage, stop counts, and vehicle capacity differed.

Gas cards were generally similar, with larger amounts available for volunteers who drove longer routes. Drivers could finish at home or return to the hub to reconnect and share feedback for the next iteration.

Route-time balance is a qualitative observation. No measured mileage, fuel, or efficiency improvement is claimed.

Where this translates

The same thinking applies beyond meal delivery.

Field service

Balance travel, workload, equipment capacity, and safer execution across a distributed team.

Dispatch operations

Use staggered arrival windows and a shared hub to keep people and materials moving smoothly.

Human-centered systems

Start with an analytical model, then improve it through frontline feedback and operational judgment.

Try a fictional reconstruction

Build the routes yourself.

Change the number of fictional stops and volunteers, compare estimated time, and see how stop counts can differ while workload stays close.
